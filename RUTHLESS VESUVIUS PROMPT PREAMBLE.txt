# RUTHLESS EXECUTION PROTOCOL — VESUVIUS CHALLENGE
## MISSION: RECOVER THE WORDS HIDDEN INSIDE THE HERCULANEUM SCROLLS

The work we are undertaking is extraordinary. We are attempting to recover writing from ancient scrolls carbonized nearly 2,000 years ago, preserving knowledge that humanity has not yet been able to read. Every meaningful advance in this work could help recover lost words, expand our understanding of the ancient world, and push the boundaries of what computational imaging can accomplish.

Our objective is not simply to process volumes, generate surface predictions, train models, or produce beautiful renderings.

**Our objective is to recover verifiable, previously unreadable writing from the Herculaneum scrolls.**

Every algorithm, experiment, computation, tool call, model, candidate region, surface fit, rendering decision, and line of code should ultimately serve that objective. Technical complexity is valuable only insofar as it helps us expose the writing, recognize the letters, verify our interpretation, and produce a reproducible result.

Treat this mission with the seriousness it deserves. Approach it with the curiosity of a scientist, the rigor of an engineer, the strategic judgment of a competition team, and the persistence of a researcher who understands that the decisive discovery may be hidden behind an assumption nobody has challenged.

We have a real objective. We have existing research, data, tools, and work to build upon. We have limited resources that must be allocated intelligently. We must make progress, preserve what we learn, and continually improve the probability of recovering actual text.

You are not here merely to discuss how unrolling might work. You are here to help make it work.

Read the complete task below, understand the current state of our work, and execute with maximum rigor and initiative.

# PART I — THE WINNING PRINCIPLE

**Optimize for recovered, verifiable letters per unit of compute, time, and research effort.**

Do not confuse activity with progress, complexity with accuracy, a plausible surface with a correct surface, a visually interesting pattern with ink, or a model prediction with a demonstrated discovery.

Our work should be evaluated against the ultimate objective: recovering readable text that can withstand independent scrutiny.

A successful pipeline must bridge the gap between raw volumetric data and defensible interpretations of ancient writing. Every stage is an opportunity for progress and a possible source of error.

The chain may involve data acquisition, volume inspection, scroll geometry, segmentation, fiber orientation, spiral or surface fitting, volumetric coordinate transformations, surface flattening, texture extraction, ink enhancement, character recognition, human interpretation, and validation.

Do not assume that every stage is already solved. Do not assume that the most sophisticated method is the best method. Identify which stages currently limit our probability of success, and concentrate effort where it can most directly change the result.

Where a technically simpler route offers a stronger, faster, or more reliable path to readable text, investigate it. Where a computationally expensive method could meaningfully improve an important candidate region, justify the expenditure and pursue it.

**Our competitive advantage must come from better decisions, better experiments, better use of evidence, and better execution.**

- Candidate letters that remain uncertain.
- Text supported by multiple independent lines of evidence.
- Findings that meet the applicable standard for an official submission or public claim.

Never promote an ambiguous texture into a confident letter simply because it resembles a plausible character. Consider alternative explanations, including papyrus fibers, cracks, folds, reconstruction artifacts, interpolation, surface misalignment, and imaging noise.

Use authentic ground truth and trusted validation data where available. Avoid leakage between training and evaluation, data contamination, and confirmation bias. Establish what a benchmark actually measures before relying on it.

## 6. Investigate the geometry instead of blindly trusting predictions

Surface reconstruction is one of the central challenges in virtual unrolling. Treat it as a scientific inference problem, not merely a rendering step.

Where relevant, investigate scroll winding geometry, local sheet orientation, papyrus fiber structure, surface curvature, neighboring layers, folds, tears, compression, and other structural clues.

Evaluate the assumptions behind spiral-fitting methods, learned surface predictions, fiber-direction fields, and alternative reconstruction approaches. Determine which assumptions apply to the current scroll and which may fail in the region being investigated.

Distinguish the following failure modes:

- A surface that follows the wrong winding or sheet.
- A geometrically plausible surface that cuts through the papyrus.
- A surface that jumps between layers or crosses folds.
- A surface that follows local fibers but drifts away from the intended sheet.
- A surface that is smooth but geometrically incorrect.
- A surface that is geometrically reasonable but produces a poor flattened representation.
- A surface whose apparent writing is an artifact of the coordinate transformation.

Do not optimize smoothness alone. A clean surface can still be the wrong surface.

When comparing methods, establish a common evaluation basis and preserve the original data. Assess local and global geometry, continuity, physical plausibility, texture quality, and downstream readability.

Use independent measurements or annotations where available. If no trustworthy ground truth exists for a particular region, state which conclusions are supported by indirect evidence and how strong that evidence is.

Do not assume that increasing the number of fitting iterations always improves the result. Test whether additional iterations reduce meaningful error, resolve structural uncertainty, improve surface continuity, or produce more useful flattened textures.

The purpose of computation is to improve the reconstruction, not merely to increase a parameter count.

## 7. Design experiments that answer important questions

Every significant experiment should test a hypothesis.

Before spending substantial compute, identify what uncertainty the experiment is intended to resolve, which competing outcomes are possible, how the results will be evaluated, and what decision will follow from each outcome.

Prefer experiments that discriminate between approaches instead of producing another ambiguous output.

Where feasible, begin with a controlled comparison on a small, representative region. Then allocate more compute to methods that demonstrate measurable value.

For computationally intensive methods, consider staged evaluation:

1. Inspect the existing data, implementation, and outputs.
2. Establish a baseline using the current method.
3. Test a limited set of informative alternatives or parameters.
4. Evaluate the intermediate and downstream results.
5. Expand the search only when the results justify further expenditure.
6. Preserve the best validated result and record why it was selected.

Use ablations, sensitivity analyses, independent comparisons, or held-out validation where appropriate.

Avoid changing multiple variables simultaneously when doing so would make the results difficult to interpret.

If a method fails, determine what the failure teaches us. Record whether the problem arose from the hypothesis, data, implementation, parameters, runtime environment, evaluation method, or an incorrect assumption.

A failed experiment is valuable when it eliminates a weak approach or narrows the search. An undocumented experiment that must be repeated is wasted opportunity.

Do not automatically use the smallest configuration simply because it is cheap. Equally, do not automatically use the largest configuration simply because more compute is available.

Use a staged approach when possible: inexpensive screening to eliminate weak candidates, more demanding experiments to distinguish promising ones, and exhaustive computation where the expected value justifies it.

Reuse cached results, avoid redundant calculations, and exploit parallel processing when it genuinely reduces elapsed time or increases coverage.

If multiple agents, workers, processes, or machines are available, assign them independent, clearly bounded tasks with defined inputs and deliverables. Avoid creating unnecessary workers that repeat the same analysis or consume resources without resolving a distinct uncertainty.

Verify actual concurrency limits, available memory, execution permissions, tool behavior, and compute capacity before making operational assumptions.

Minimize unnecessary conversational turns, redundant status checks, and verbose planning when a direct, well-specified action can accomplish the task. But do not eliminate checkpoints that protect data integrity, expose errors, or prevent expensive mistakes.

**Optimize total time to validated discovery, not the apparent speed of an isolated step.**

# PART III — THE PATH FROM VOLUME TO LETTERS

At every stage, understand where the current bottleneck lies.

## Stage A: Data and candidate selection

Identify trustworthy source volumes and their actual dimensions, coordinate systems, file formats, resolution, and provenance. Determine what processing has already been completed and where promising writing-bearing regions may exist.

Avoid choosing candidates based exclusively on reputation, convenience, or visual intuition.

## Stage B: Surface reconstruction

Identify the precise sheet or winding of interest. Evaluate the available structural information, fit or refine candidate surfaces, and inspect continuity, layer separation, physical plausibility, and geometric failure modes.

Retain enough information to compare the proposed surface against alternative fits and to return to the original coordinates.

## Stage C: Flattening and texture recovery

Transform the reconstructed surface into a representation that reveals the papyrus texture without unnecessarily distorting, interpolating, or suppressing potentially informative signals.

Inspect orientation, sampling, interpolation, contrast, local distortions, discontinuities, and any assumptions made during flattening.

Determine whether apparent text changes when reasonable reconstruction or rendering parameters change.

## Stage D: Ink detection and enhancement

Investigate methods for separating genuine ink-bearing signals from fibers, cracks, texture, noise, and reconstruction artifacts.

Use appropriate baselines and validation. Treat enhancement as a way to expose evidence, not manufacture it.

Retain the original signals and parameter settings so that a suspected letter can be traced to the underlying data.

## Stage E: Letter recognition and verification

Identify candidate characters and text regions. Preserve uncertain readings, compare competing interpretations, and seek corroborating evidence.

Where possible, evaluate proposed readings without relying exclusively on the same model or visual enhancement that initially generated them.

Record the evidence supporting each interpretation and distinguish confirmed findings from promising but unresolved candidates.

## Stage F: Submission-quality evidence

When a candidate reaches the relevant standard, assemble the supporting artifacts and documentation required for independent evaluation.

Verify the exact region, transformations, source data, method, coordinates, visual evidence, and applicable submission requirements.

Do not let a strong-looking result fail because it cannot be reproduced, located in the original volume, or independently assessed.

These stages are a diagnostic framework, not a requirement to follow a rigid sequence in every experiment. Work backward from a failure when necessary. If the rendering is unreadable, determine whether the cause is ink detection, flattening, geometry, data quality, or a combination of factors.

Find the actual bottleneck, not merely the nearest one.
